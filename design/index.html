A dashboard for heat and gas

Three screens, drawn to the 7″ panel and sized for desktop and phone. Below them, the two pieces I was least sure about — the heating diagram and the gas chart — as options. Rings and bars were chosen, and the build is running.

02

The heating diagram

This is the piece I am least sure about, so here are three ways to draw it. All three carry the same information: the boiler, the outside temperature, the boost, and the rooms.

A  Rings Chosen

Your home in the middle, everything else around it. The house is the hub; the ring around it fills toward the target temperature. Siblings of the FoxESS dashboard, so it will feel familiar, and it stays calm when the boiler is off — no animation, no flicker.

Costs the most SVG work, but it is the only one that scales down gracefully to a phone.

B  House cutaway

A plan of the house with each room shaded by warmth and the boiler glowing in the corner. The most charming of the three, and the one that answers “where in the house is the heat?” at a glance.

But it assumes a floor plan, and it is the hardest to make legible at 800×480 and hopeless on a phone. It would need a drawing, not just data.

C  Linear flow

Outside, boiler, home, boost — left to right, with the dashes moving the way the heat does. Reads like a sentence, and it is the easiest of the three to make responsive because it just stacks.

Less glanceable than rings, and the rooms have nowhere to go. Good as the phone layout, not as the main one.

As built Built

The rings got bigger, because the first version was too tight to hold what it was saying: home at r 56, the boiler and the outside at r 38, boost at r 24. The state text now sits inside its ring instead of hanging off the bottom of it, where it ran out of room.

Each ring carries a symbol, and the symbol has to mean what the ring says: a flame for a firing boiler, a snowflake below 5°C, a sun above 20°C, a plain thermometer in between. The boost ring counts the minutes actually left rather than a hardcoded half hour.

The status tile's “next schedule block” row is gone — Ember does not read Hive's schedule, so it could only ever show a dash. In its place: “a day, on average”, the mean heating time over the last seven whole days.

03

The gas chart

The 24-hour bar chart is the wrong shape for this data. Octopus delivers gas a day or two late, so “the last 24 hours” is usually empty — and on the days when it is not empty it is showing a day that ended two days ago. Three alternatives, drawn with the real numbers: 2.43 m³ a day, a meter exchange on 3 November, and today with no reading at all.

Decided, and built: A on the gas panel, B on the Today panel, C behind the MONTH tab. The tabs are real now — DAY, WEEK and MONTH each draw something different, and each says what it is showing.

A   A week of days Built

One column per day for the last seven days, the height of it being that day's total, with a line for the weekly average and today's estimate in plum. It is always full, it is the unit people think in — a day's gas — and it sits beside the “7-day average” and “vs average” tiles without repeating them.

A day with no readings is estimated, in plum, with the band's whisker over the top — the same model that forecasts tomorrow. A day with part of a reading keeps that part in teal, faded, under the estimate. The part is real and the rest is a guess, so the two are never the same colour.

B   The shape of a day Built

The same 24 hours as the old chart, but each column is the average use in that hour across the last week rather than one night's readings. It is never empty, and it answers a question the old chart could not: when does this house use gas. The cold morning and the warm evening are visible at a glance.

Best on the Today panel, where it explains where today's gas goes rather than how much of it there is.

C   The month Built

A calendar grid of the last 30 days, each day shaded by what it used. It carries the month budget, it shows the meter exchange as a visible hole rather than a mystery, and it is the view that answers “is this month normal?” — the question a 24-hour chart could never ask.

This was the direction chosen in the first round. The missing piece was ever having the history to draw it, and that is now fixed: Ember asks Octopus for a month of readings when it starts.

The decision

A on the gas panel and B on the Today panel, with C behind the MONTH tab. A is the “how much” the gas slide is for; B is the “when” that the Today panel is missing. C is the long view, and it is the one that will matter in February.

Chosen, and built.

The tabs are real

The tabs on the gas panel used to be decoration — only 24H drew anything. They now switch between three views, each the shape that suits it, and each naming itself in the panel heading: DAY the shape of a day, WEEK a week of days, MONTH the month.

Whatever is chosen, the chart says what it is showing. “Last 24 hours” on a panel that is showing the last two days is a lie, and the axis was the thing making it.

One rule runs through all three: a day the meter has not reported is never drawn as a zero. Zero means no gas was used, which would be a lie. What the day is depends on what Ember can honestly say about it: the week chart estimates it, because the chart is asking how much gas each day took; the month grid leaves it a hole, because the calendar is asking what the meter actually said, and the meter exchange belongs in that answer.

04

Hive Insights

The app shows heating hours, a heating history and a budget. None of that came out of the API — it comes out of history Ember keeps itself. Here is exactly where each one comes from, and what Hive+ would charge you for the same thing.

01
Heating hours
The API reports the boiler as firing or not, right now. It does not report “6 hours 20 minutes today”. That number is a sum over time, so Ember has to keep its own record: poll the boiler state, store the transitions, and add them up. The app does this in Hive’s cloud; we do it on the Pi.
Build it — it needs the history store
02
Heating history and trends
Same raw material: every state change is recorded with a timestamp, and the history is the list of them. Hive also exposes a per-product events endpoint, which may hand us the same transitions without polling. Worth knowing: the free app already has a limited Heating History — Hive+ unlocks the full one, and with TRVs it can break the history down per room as well as per day.
Build it from stored history
03
Budget tracking
A budget is a number you set, so there is nothing to fetch — but there is something to measure it against, and that is where Ember is better than the app. Hive budgets against the boiler’s own estimate. Ember budgets against your metered gas and your actual tariff rate, so the number is the money you will really pay. Hive’s own Budget Tracker is a Hive+ feature — this one is free and local.
Build it — on metered gas, not an estimate
04
What Hive+ would cost you
Hive+ is £3.99 a month or £39.90 a year, and it is where Insights, the full Heating History and the Budget Tracker live. But three of its headline features — Saver Mode, Schedule Assist and Away Mode — are explicitly not compatible with Hive radiator valves. You have TRVs. The subscription would buy you the least relevant half of itself.
Skip it — Ember does it locally, free
05
Why our forecast can be better than Hive’s
Hive needs a British Gas smart meter account linked before it can show you spending, and it says outright that a new system needs a minimum 30-day learning period before it will forecast anything. Ember fits a degree-day model to your own gas readings, so it can put a number on tomorrow from the first week — with a deliberately wide band until it has enough history to trust.
Build it — and be honest about the band
05

Colour and type

Four colours, one for each thing that can happen in a house. Ember for heat, ice for outside, teal for gas, plum for anything predicted — and everything else is grey so the numbers stay the loudest thing on the screen.

Three kinds of number, and they never mix

2.43m³
Measured — what the meter actually reported. Teal, no adjective.
2.41m³
Estimated — what today should use, from today's weather and your own past readings. Plum, and the word estimated.
2.52m³
Forecast — what tomorrow should use. Plum, and the word forecast.

Octopus does not deliver gas as it happens, so today has no measured gas in it at all. Ember does not leave a hole — it estimates today from the same model that forecasts tomorrow, shows the band, and says estimated. A measured figure is never replaced by one: the moment the meter reports, the estimate goes away.

07

What I need from you

01
Which heating diagram?
A (rings) is my recommendation — it belongs to the same family as the FoxESS dashboard, it stays calm when nothing is happening, and it survives a narrow screen. B (house cutaway) is the loveliest and needs a floor plan. C (linear) is the fallback for phones.
Pick A, and C on phones
02
Which gas chart?
A (bars) for the live 24-hour view, with C (month heatmap) as the 30-day tab beside it. B (area) is the alternative if you would rather see the week's shape than its hours.
Pick A, with C as the 30-day tab
03
Are these colours right?
Ember for heat, teal for measured gas, ice for outside, plum for anything predicted. The name sets the accent, but if your kitchen reads better in a cooler palette I would not argue.
Pick as shown, or name one to change
04
What fills the space hot water used to have?
With a combi, Hive has no hot water state at all — so today’s gas cannot be split into “hot water” and “radiators”. The dashboard will show total gas and explain it with the heating state instead. The space that freed up could take “this week so far”, or the cost as money, or nothing at all.
Pick: total gas only, and leave the space clean
08

Setting Ember up

Built after the first build. The original plan was that you would run a probe in a terminal and copy a file to the right place. That works, once, and then it breaks — because it is two steps and one of them is remembering. So the settings page does the discovering instead.

01
You give it the two things you actually have
Your Octopus API key and your Hive login. Everything else is worked out by talking to the services: the account number, the meter point, every meter on it, the product and tariff codes, the meter’s own type, the rates, and the unit the meter reads in. Your postcode is enough for the weather — Open-Meteo turns it into a latitude and longitude and needs no key of its own.
Build it — ask for the key, find the rest
02
The SMS code
Hive may send a one-time code to your phone. The phone is the only place that code exists, so nothing can work it out for you — but it can be typed into a box on the page, which is friendlier than a terminal prompt. The session is written where Ember can refresh it unattended from then on, and the device is remembered so the code is not asked for every time.
Build it — a box on the page for the code
03
What the browser remembers
The form is kept in the browser’s local storage, so a reload does not throw away what you typed. The settings Ember is really using live in a file written 0600, and the page always shows those in preference to the remembered ones. Once a sign-in has worked, the secret is taken back out of the browser — it lives in the file now.
Build it — local storage for the form, a file for the truth
04
No login
The settings page can hold your Hive password, so it would be the obvious thing to put a login on. But this is your own network, and a shared password is not a login — it is just a secret written on a Post-it. So there is none, and the page says so plainly. Keep it on a network you trust.
No login — private network only
05
The probes stay
They are still the sharpest way to see what an account really contains, and they are what to run when the shape of an API moves. But they are diagnostics now, not setup. The probe and the app share the same sign-in code, so the two cannot disagree about whether the handshake is right — which matters more than it sounds, because a wrong password and a broken handshake produce exactly the same error message.
Keep them — as diagnostics, sharing the app’s code
